## Mark Ferrari's Dithering Technique

Mark Ferrari pioneered checkerboard dithering to create more useful colors, and the CRT monitors of the time were blurry enough that the checkerboard dither blurred into a solid third color. He was working with only 16 pre-selected colors that had been chosen by programmers using equally-spaced RGB intervals, resulting in horribly acid, unnatural colors. 

Initially, programmers told him dithering couldn't compress, but after Ferrari left a dithered twilight landscape on his monitor as a protest, the team worked out how to make dither compressible within two months.

## Color Cycling

Ferrari became world-renowned for his color cycling and palette shifting techniques, which could be used to make a single computer illustration appear alive. According to Ferrari, "color cycling creates the illusion of animated movement in pretty much the same way light bulbs on a theater marquee do," with each pixel changing colors in a looping sequence to create the illusion of moving bands of color.

## Living Worlds

The Living Worlds app revived a collection of beloved, classic pixel art from Mark Ferrari. Living Worlds scenes are windows into worlds that feel alive and change throughout the day, using pixel art with color palette tricks like color cycling and palette fading.

## Key Shadow Elements on a Sphere

The different elements of light include the Terminator (where light transitions into shadow), Form Shadow (the area beyond the terminator turned away from the light source), and Cast Shadow (which occurs when an object blocks light from hitting another surface).

## Shadow Values and Structure

In the shadows, artists use two values: first block in the reflected light (value 4) as the lighter shadow value, then add the darker core shadow (1-2). Core Shadow is the darkest part of the form shadow, usually along the terminator.

## Reflected Light

Reflected Light is light bouncing from the environment into the shadow, lightening it slightly. Light from the source can't directly reach the underside of the sphere, but it can reach the surrounding surfaces, bounce up, and lighten the bottom of the form, which is called reflected light.

## Occlusion Shadow

Occlusion Shadow is the darkest value in tight crevices where objects touch. The occlusion shadow sits immediately below the form and is the darkest part of the cast shadow because it's least affected by both the light source and reflected light.

# Mark Ferrari's Painting Technique Summary

## Palette Planning
Ferrari practiced extreme economy with the 256-color indexed palette. He reserved space for future features and never "expanded into extra available palette space if it wasn't clearly needed." Multiple palettes enabled seamless transitions—a single artwork could shift through nine different palettes to represent different times of day and weather conditions simultaneously.

**Key quote:** "Change the palette, and get a picture containing completely different content as well as different light and color."

## Dithering Strategy
His choice of dithering algorithm was deliberate and artistic. He favored patterned dither for smooth, precise effects requiring evenly-spaced color shifts. However, he strategically switched to diffusion dither for chaotic textures (like foam) and used no-dither settings for solid elements like snow and rain.

**Key quote:** "Different effects work better or worse with different dithering methods."

## Form and Light
Ferrari created the illusion of moving light and shadow by layering cycling color gradients. Light and shadow would "gradually change angle, climb down the sides of things, move across lawns, up cliffs or building walls" purely through palette transitions—without modifying the underlying pixel art.

## Water and Reflections
Moving water effects used cycling gradients applied over carefully arranged pixels. Similar techniques created wavering reflections in scenes like the Mountain Mirror Pond.

## Foliage and Movement
He outlined leaves with contrasting colors, then filled edges with moving gradients matching alternating leaf and background colors. This created the illusion of leaves swaying while preventing rigid, synchronized motion through syncopated gradient fills.

**Key quote:** "The 'core leaf shapes' would seem to expand INTO the bands of moving color that matched the leaf, and shrink AWAY from the bands of color that matched the leaf shape's surroundings."

## Tools and Time
Ferrari created these images in Deluxe Paint, mastering features—stencils, gradient fills, palette management—in ways "no one had intended or foreseen." Each artwork required 3-21 days of 8-14 hour workdays depending on complexity.

# Shading Light and Form – Basics Summary

## Core Value Structure

- **Two main zones**: "Light and shadow. The edge where the form transitions from light to shadow is the terminator."
- Start by separating lights and darks into distinct value families before adding transitions

## Key Elements

**Terminator**

- Boundary where planes turn away from the light source
- Located at the tangent between light source and form

**Halftone**

- Planes partially hit by direct light
- Sits immediately after the terminator in the light zone
- Gets progressively lighter as planes face the light more directly

**Core Shadow**

- Darker plane defining the shadow edge
- Visibility depends on reflected light angle and form thickness
- Many artists add it for dimensional effect even when subtle

**Reflected Light**

- Bounce light entering shadows from surrounding surfaces
- Never reaches the brightness of halftone areas
- Strengthens with more reflective nearby surfaces

**Cast Shadow**

- Blocked light on another form
- Can be intentionally shaped to describe the surface receiving it rather than the form casting it

**Occlusion Shadow**

- Deepest shadows where forms meet (e.g., egg-to-table junction)
- Receives minimal reflected light

## Artistic Freedom

- Highlights and cast shadows can be repositioned while maintaining believability
- Learn technical rules, then strategically break them for improved drawings

"""Atelier core: a tiny numpy SDF ray-marcher plus the value/dither tools.

Everything here is about FORM and VALUE. Colour and material live elsewhere.
"""
import os
os.environ.setdefault("OMP_NUM_THREADS", "4")
os.environ.setdefault("OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS", "4")
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- vectors
def norm(v):
    v = np.asarray(v, dtype=np.float64)
    return v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=-1, keepdims=True)

def dot(a, b):
    return np.sum(a * b, axis=-1)

def sun_dir(azimuth_deg, elevation_deg):
    """azimuth measured in the camera frame: 0 = light from the LEFT (travelling +x),
    90 = from behind the viewer (front light), 180 = from the right, 270 = from behind
    the subject (back-light). Returns the unit vector pointing TOWARD the sun."""
    a = np.radians(azimuth_deg); e = np.radians(elevation_deg)
    # toward-sun vector: left sun sits at -x
    x = -np.cos(a) * np.cos(e)
    z = -np.sin(a) * np.cos(e)
    y = np.sin(e)
    return norm([x, y, z])

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- SDF primitives
def sd_sphere(p, c, r):
    return np.linalg.norm(p - c, axis=-1) - r

def sd_box(p, c, b, r=0.0):
    q = np.abs(p - c) - (np.asarray(b) - r)
    return np.linalg.norm(np.maximum(q, 0), axis=-1) + np.minimum(q.max(-1), 0) - r

def sd_cyl(p, c, r, h, rr=0.0):
    q = p - c
    d = np.stack([np.hypot(q[..., 0], q[..., 2]) - (r - rr), np.abs(q[..., 1]) - (h - rr)], -1)
    return np.minimum(d.max(-1), 0) + np.linalg.norm(np.maximum(d, 0), axis=-1) - rr

def sd_ellipsoid(p, c, r):
    q = (p - c) / np.asarray(r)
    k0 = np.linalg.norm(q, axis=-1)
    k1 = np.linalg.norm(q / np.asarray(r), axis=-1)
    return k0 * (k0 - 1.0) / np.maximum(k1, 1e-9)

def smin(a, b, k):
    h = np.clip(0.5 + 0.5 * (b - a) / k, 0, 1)
    return b * (1 - h) + a * h - k * h * (1 - h)

def smax(a, b, k):
    return -smin(-a, -b, k)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- camera
class Camera:
    def __init__(self, W, H, pos, target, fov_deg=50.0):
        self.W, self.H = W, H
        self.pos = np.asarray(pos, float)
        f = norm(np.asarray(target, float) - self.pos)
        r = norm(np.cross(f, [0, 1, 0]))  # right-handed: x right, y up, z forward
        r = -r if r[0] < 0 and f[2] > 0 else r
        u = np.cross(r, f)
        self.f, self.r, self.u = f, r, u
        self.tanh = np.tan(np.radians(fov_deg) / 2)

    def rays(self):
        W, H = self.W, self.H
        xs = (np.arange(W) + 0.5) / W * 2 - 1
        ys = 1 - (np.arange(H) + 0.5) / H * 2
        X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs * self.tanh, ys * self.tanh * H / W)
        d = self.f + X[..., None] * self.r + Y[..., None] * self.u
        d = norm(d)
        o = np.broadcast_to(self.pos, d.shape).copy()
        return o, d

    def project(self, P):
        """world point(s) -> pixel coords (x, y), depth"""
        v = np.asarray(P, float) - self.pos
        z = dot(v, self.f)
        x = dot(v, self.r) / z / self.tanh
        y = dot(v, self.u) / z / (self.tanh * self.H / self.W)
        return (x + 1) / 2 * self.W, (1 - y) / 2 * self.H, z

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- marching
def march(scene, o, d, tmax=400.0, steps=220, eps=1e-3, relax=0.9):
    """scene(p) -> (dist, matid). Vectorised over the leading shape."""
    shp = o.shape[:-1]
    o = o.reshape(-1, 3); d = d.reshape(-1, 3)
    t = np.zeros(len(o)); alive = np.ones(len(o), bool); hit = np.zeros(len(o), bool)
    for _ in range(steps):
        idx = np.nonzero(alive)[0]
        if len(idx) == 0:
            break
        p = o[idx] + t[idx, None] * d[idx]
        dist, _ = scene(p)
        th = eps * np.maximum(1.0, t[idx] * 0.02)
        h = dist < th
        hit[idx[h]] = True
        t[idx] += np.where(h, 0, dist * relax)
        far = t[idx] > tmax
        alive[idx[h | far]] = False
    p = o + t[:, None] * d
    mat = np.full(len(o), -1)
    if hit.any():
        _, m = scene(p[hit]); mat[hit] = m
    return t.reshape(shp), hit.reshape(shp), p.reshape(shp + (3,)), mat.reshape(shp)

def normals(scene, p, e=2e-3):
    shp = p.shape
    p = p.reshape(-1, 3)
    n = np.zeros_like(p)
    for i in range(3):
        dv = np.zeros(3); dv[i] = e
        n[:, i] = scene(p + dv)[0] - scene(p - dv)[0]
    return norm(n).reshape(shp)

def soft_shadow(scene, p, L, k=24.0, tmin=0.02, tmax=60.0, steps=120):
    """1 = fully lit, 0 = in cast shadow. k controls penumbra hardness."""
    shp = p.shape[:-1]
    p = p.reshape(-1, 3)
    res = np.ones(len(p)); t = np.full(len(p), tmin); alive = np.ones(len(p), bool)
    L = np.broadcast_to(L, p.shape)
    for _ in range(steps):
        idx = np.nonzero(alive)[0]
        if len(idx) == 0:
            break
        h, _ = scene(p[idx] + t[idx, None] * L[idx])
        res[idx] = np.minimum(res[idx], k * h / t[idx])
        t[idx] += np.clip(h, 0.01, 1.0)
        dead = (h < 1e-4) | (t[idx] > tmax)
        res[idx[h < 1e-4]] = 0
        alive[idx[dead]] = False
    return np.clip(res, 0, 1).reshape(shp)

def ambient_occ(scene, p, n, scale=0.12, n_s=5):
    shp = p.shape[:-1]
    p = p.reshape(-1, 3); n = n.reshape(-1, 3)
    occ = np.zeros(len(p)); w = 1.0
    for i in range(1, n_s + 1):
        h = scale * i
        d, _ = scene(p + n * h)
        occ += w * np.maximum(h - d, 0)
        w *= 0.6
    return np.clip(1 - occ / scale * 0.9, 0, 1).reshape(shp)

BAYER4 = np.array([[0, 8, 2, 10], [12, 4, 14, 6], [3, 11, 1, 9], [15, 7, 13, 5]]) / 16.0 + 1 / 32

def threshold_map(H, W, kind="bayer4"):
    m = BAYER4 if kind == "bayer4" else CHECK
    reps = (H // m.shape[0] + 1, W // m.shape[1] + 1)
    return np.tile(m, reps)[:H, :W]

def quantize(v, levels, dither=True, kind="bayer4", strength=1.0):
    """v in [0,1] continuous -> integer level 0..levels-1 with ordered dither
    only ever mixing two ADJACENT levels (the pixel-artist's rule)."""
    x = np.clip(v, 0, 1) * (levels - 1)
    if not dither:
        return np.clip(np.round(x), 0, levels - 1).astype(int)
    lo = np.floor(x); fr = x - lo
    # compress the dither band: only the middle `strength` part of each step is dithered
    s = max(strength, 1e-6)
    fr = np.clip((fr - 0.5) / s + 0.5, 0, 1)
    T = threshold_map(*v.shape, kind)
    return np.clip(lo + (fr > T), 0, levels - 1).astype(int)

"""The atelier value model: light family / shadow family, kept apart.

continuous value v in [0,1]:
  light family  : [LF_LO, 1]   halftone near the terminator, lightest facing the light
  shadow family : [0, SF_HI]   core near the terminator, reflected light toward the bounce,
                               occlusion darkest where forms meet
The gap between SF_HI and LF_LO is the whole point: the two families never touch,
so the form reads in two values from across the room before any modelling.
"""
import numpy as np
from core import dot, norm

LF_LO, SF_HI = 0.58, 0.36

def bounce_dir(L):
    """Toward-the-bounce direction: from below, and from the side away from the light."""
    h = np.array([-L[0], 0.0, -L[2]])
    n = np.linalg.norm(h)
    h = h / n if n > 1e-6 else h
    return norm(h * 0.8 + np.array([0, -0.6, 0]))

def form_value(N, L, shadow, ao, *, light_gamma=0.75, core=0.10, bounce=0.16,
               shadow_base=0.17, ao_power=1.6, occ_floor=0.0):
    ndl = dot(N, L)
    lit_amt = np.clip(ndl, 0, 1) * shadow                     # direct light actually received
    lit = (ndl > 0) & (shadow > 0.5)
    v_light = LF_LO + (1 - LF_LO) * np.clip(ndl, 0, 1) ** light_gamma
    B = bounce_dir(L)
    refl = np.clip(dot(N, B), 0, 1)
    v_shadow = shadow_base + bounce * refl
    # core shadow: darkest band just past the terminator (faces neither light nor bounce)
    v_shadow -= core * np.exp(-((ndl + 0.12) / 0.14) ** 2) * (ndl < 0.05)
    v_shadow = np.minimum(v_shadow, SF_HI)
    v = np.where(lit, v_light, v_shadow)
    # occlusion darkens both families; in shadow it goes toward black (contact)
    v = v * (ao ** ao_power)
    return np.clip(v, occ_floor, 1), lit, lit_amt

"""Geometry for the task: the boulder mass and the bank/water setting (as SDFs).

The boulder is designed the way the atelier teaches you to draw one:

  1. a big simple mass (a squat, slightly lopsided ellipsoid, the 'loaf'),
  2. broken into a few large PLANES (a convex set of cutting planes, rounded at the
     edges by weathering: granite exfoliates in shells, so edges round and faces stay broad),
  3. only then small irregularity (low-frequency lumps), and
  4. a couple of joint cracks cut into it.
"""
import numpy as np
from core import norm, dot, sd_ellipsoid, smin, smax

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ noise
def _hash3(ix, iy, iz, seed):
    h = (ix * 374761393 + iy * 668265263 + iz * 1274126177 + seed * 1442695041) & 0xFFFFFFFF
    h = ((h ^ (h >> 13)) * 1274126177) & 0xFFFFFFFF
    return ((h ^ (h >> 16)) & 0xFFFF) / 65535.0

def vnoise3(p, seed=0):
    """smooth value noise in [-1, 1]"""
    fl = np.floor(p); f = p - fl
    i = fl.astype(np.int64)
    u = f * f * (3 - 2 * f)
    out = 0
    for dx in (0, 1):
        for dy in (0, 1):
            for dz in (0, 1):
                w = (u[..., 0] if dx else 1 - u[..., 0]) * (u[..., 1] if dy else 1 - u[..., 1]) * \
                    (u[..., 2] if dz else 1 - u[..., 2])
                out = out + w * _hash3(i[..., 0] + dx, i[..., 1] + dy, i[..., 2] + dz, seed)
    return out * 2 - 1

def fbm3(p, seed=0, octaves=3, lac=2.03, gain=0.5):
    a, s, tot = 1.0, 0.0, 0.0
    for o in range(octaves):
        s = s + a * vnoise3(p, seed + o * 17)
        tot += a; a *= gain; p = p * lac
    return s / tot

def vnoise2(x, y, seed=0):
    return vnoise3(np.stack([x, y, np.zeros_like(x) + 0.37], -1), seed)

def fbm2(x, y, seed=0, octaves=3):
    return fbm3(np.stack([x, y, np.zeros_like(x) + 0.37], -1), seed, octaves)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ boulder
class Boulder:
    def __init__(self, size=1.8, seed=0, center=(0, 0, 0), sink=0.28, n_planes=11,
                 round_k=None, lump=0.035, cracks=2):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.seed = seed
        self.size = size
        s = size / 2
        # the loaf: wider than tall, a bit deeper than wide, lopsided
        self.r = np.array([s * rng.uniform(1.0, 1.15), s * rng.uniform(0.66, 0.8), s * rng.uniform(0.85, 1.0)])
        self.c = np.array(center, float) + np.array([0, self.r[1] * (1 - 2 * sink), 0])
        yaw = rng.uniform(0, np.pi)
        self.rot = np.array([[np.cos(yaw), 0, np.sin(yaw)], [0, 1, 0], [-np.sin(yaw), 0, np.cos(yaw)]])
        # cutting planes: normals spread over the upper hemisphere and sides
        ns, offs = [], []
        for i in range(n_planes):
            th = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi)
            el = rng.uniform(-0.25, 1.0) if i else 1.0                 # first plane = the top
            n = norm([np.cos(th) * np.sqrt(1 - el * el) * 1.0, el, np.sin(th) * np.sqrt(1 - el * el)])
            # how deep it cuts: the ellipsoid support distance, shaved by 8..22 %
            sup = np.sqrt(np.sum((self.r * n) ** 2))
            ns.append(n); offs.append(sup * rng.uniform(0.78, 0.92) if i else sup * rng.uniform(0.80, 0.88))
        self.ns = np.array(ns); self.offs = np.array(offs)
        self.k = round_k if round_k is not None else 0.16 * s
        self.lump = lump * size
        # cracks: thin slabs through the mass, nearly vertical
        self.cracks = []
        for i in range(cracks):
            th = rng.uniform(0, np.pi)
            n = norm([np.cos(th), rng.uniform(-0.3, 0.3), np.sin(th)])
            self.cracks.append((n, rng.uniform(-0.35, 0.35) * s, rng.uniform(0.012, 0.02) * size))

    def local(self, p):
        return (p - self.c) @ self.rot   # into boulder frame

    def sdf(self, p):
        q = self.local(p)
        d = sd_ellipsoid(q, np.zeros(3), self.r)

        return d

    def __init__(self, size="medium", light="left", dist="10m", seed=0, sun_elev=9.0):

def make_camera(st, P):
    b = st.boulder
    eye_h = max(1.5, st.lip + P.D * np.tan(np.radians(10)) + b.size * 0.35)
    pos = st.cam_h + np.array([0, eye_h, 0])
    # aim at the boulder, then swing a little so it sits off-centre (left third)
    tgt = b.c + np.array([0, 0.0, 0])
    f = norm(tgt - pos); fh = norm([f[0], 0, f[2]])

class Backdrop:
    """Sky, a far mountain wall and a far treeline, as a function of ray DIRECTION only.
    Because it depends on direction only, the water's reflection of it is automatically the
    mirror image about the horizon, as it is for distant things."""

        eye = cam.pos

        self.tree_h = np.arctan2(9.0, st.far + st.D)        # ~9 m spruces on the far bank
        self.cam = cam

    def angles(self, d):

        el = np.arcsin(np.clip(d[..., 1], -1, 1))

"""The painter: buffers -> indexed pixel art.

Every material owns a RAMP: an ordered list of palette entries from dark to light.
Painting happens in 'step space' (a float position along the material's ramp); marks
(grass strokes, granite flecks, ripple lines, edge accents) are offsets in step space;
quantising mixes only two ADJACENT entries of ONE ramp, with a checker dither.
Stage 4 uses grey ramps (value only). Stage 5 swaps in colour ramps of the SAME values.
"""
from core import *
from scene import SKY, MTN, TREES, LAND, BOULDER, WATER
from geometry import fbm3, fbm2

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ ramps (values)
# luminance targets per ramp entry, dark -> light. The colour stage keeps these values.
RAMP_LUM = {
    "sky":   [0.60, 0.67, 0.74, 0.81, 0.87, 0.93],
    "mtn":   [0.34, 0.42, 0.52, 0.63, 0.75, 0.86],   # shadow rock .. lit snow
    "trees": [0.05, 0.10, 0.17, 0.26],
    "grass": [0.06, 0.12, 0.20, 0.30, 0.42, 0.54, 0.66],
    "rock":  [0.07, 0.15, 0.25, 0.48, 0.66, 0.84],
    "water": [0.22, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.70],   # the tarn itself: reflected sky, darkened
}
RAMP_ORDER = ["sky", "mtn", "trees", "grass", "rock", "water"]

def palette_layout(ramps=RAMP_LUM):
    off, k = {}, 0
    for r in RAMP_ORDER:
        off[r] = k; k += len(ramps[r])
    return off, k

def grey_palette(ramps=RAMP_LUM):
    cols = []
    for r in RAMP_ORDER:
        for l in ramps[r]:
            g = round(l * 255); cols.append((g, g, g))
    return np.array(cols)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ helpers
def hash2(x, y, s):
    h = (x * 73856093) ^ (y * 19349663) ^ (s * 83492791)
    h = (h ^ (h >> 13)) * 1274126177
    return ((h ^ (h >> 16)) & 0xFFFF) / 65535.0

def shift(a, dy, dx, fill):
    out = np.full_like(a, fill)
    H, W = a.shape[:2]
    ys, yd = (slice(0, H - dy), slice(dy, H)) if dy >= 0 else (slice(-dy, H), slice(0, H + dy))
    xs, xd = (slice(0, W - dx), slice(dx, W)) if dx >= 0 else (slice(-dx, W), slice(0, W + dx))
    out[yd, xd] = a[ys, xs]
    return out

class Painter:
    def __init__(self, buf, seed=0, ramps=RAMP_LUM, marks=True):
        self.b = buf; self.seed = seed; self.ramps = ramps; self.marks = marks
        self.H, self.W = buf["mat"].shape
        self.off, self.n = palette_layout(ramps)
        yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:self.H, 0:self.W]
        self.yy, self.xx = yy, xx

    # -------------------------------------------------------------- base steps
    def material_steps(self, v, mat):
        """value-model output -> (ramp name per pixel as int code, step float)"""
        H, W = mat.shape
        code = np.full((H, W), -1); step = np.zeros((H, W))
        R = {n: i for i, n in enumerate(RAMP_ORDER)}
        m = mat == SKY;     code[m] = R["sky"];   step[m] = (v[m] - 0.60) / (0.95 - 0.60) * 5
        m = mat == MTN;     code[m] = R["mtn"];   step[m] = (v[m] - 0.36) / (0.80 - 0.36) * 5
        m = mat == TREES;   code[m] = R["trees"]; step[m] = (v[m] - 0.10) / (0.28 - 0.10) * 3
        m = mat == LAND;    code[m] = R["grass"]; step[m] = np.interp(v[m] * 5, np.arange(6), [0.3, 1.2, 2.1, 3.6, 4.7, 5.8])
        m = mat == BOULDER; code[m] = R["rock"];  step[m] = v[m] * 5
        return code, step

    def base(self):
        b = self.b
        code, step = self.material_steps(b["v"], b["mat"])
        # aerial perspective: distant land & rock lose contrast toward a mid step
        d = np.clip(b["depth"], 0, 1e3)
        far = 1 - np.exp(-d / 70.0)
        R = {n: i for i, n in enumerate(RAMP_ORDER)}
        g = code == R["grass"]; r = code == R["rock"]
        step[g] = step[g] + (3.6 - step[g]) * 0.55 * far[g]
        step[r] = step[r] + (3.2 - step[r]) * 0.55 * far[r]
        fl = np.clip((d - 8) / 40, 0, 0.55)
        step[g] = np.where(step[g] < 3.6, step[g] + (3.6 - step[g]) * fl[g], step[g])
        # water: the reflected thing, darker, broken by ripples (painted in marks)
        wcode, wstep = self.water_steps()
        w = b["water"]
        code[w] = wcode[w]; step[w] = wstep[w]
        self.code, self.step = code, step
        return code, step

    def water_steps(self, rv=None, rmat=None):
        b = self.b
        rv = b["rv"] if rv is None else rv
        rmat = b["rmat"] if rmat is None else rmat
        code, step = self.material_steps(rv, rmat)
        R = {n: i for i, n in enumerate(RAMP_ORDER)}
        # Fresnel: grazing water mirrors, water seen from above shows its dark body
        cos_i = np.abs(b["dwy"])
        dark = 1.6 * (1 - np.exp(-cos_i / 0.16))                     # steps of darkening
        # sky & mountains reflect into the WATER ramp (by value), the rest into their own
        sky_like = np.isin(rmat, [SKY, MTN])
        lum = np.zeros_like(step)
        for name in ["sky", "mtn"]:
            m = code == R[name]
            lum[m] = np.interp(step[m], np.arange(len(self.ramps[name])), self.ramps[name])
        wl = np.array(self.ramps["water"])

        code = np.where(sky_like, R["water"], code)
        step = np.where(sky_like, wstep, step - 0.9 - dark * 0.8)
        return code, step

    def water_marks(self):
        """broken reflection: rows wobble sideways a little (more toward the viewer), and
        thin horizontal ripple lines lie on the surface -- longer near, shorter far."""
        b = self.b; H, W = self.H, self.W
        w = b["water"]

        # wobble: sample the reflection from a neighbouring column
        amp = np.round(np.sin(self.yy * 0.9 + self.seed) * np.sin(self.yy * 0.23 + 1.7) * (0.6 + 2.0 * near)).astype(int)
        xs = np.clip(self.xx + amp, 0, W - 1)
        src_w = w[self.yy, xs]
        rv2 = np.where(src_w, b["rv"][self.yy, xs], b["rv"])
        rm2 = np.where(src_w, b["rmat"][self.yy, xs], b["rmat"])
        c2, s2 = self.water_steps(rv2, rm2)
        self.code[w] = c2[w]; self.step[w] = s2[w]
        # ripple lines: horizontal dashes of +/- a step
        seglen = np.clip(3 + 22 * near, 3, 26)
        seg = np.floor((self.xx + hash2(self.yy, 7, self.seed) * 50) / seglen).astype(int)
        r = hash2(seg, self.yy, self.seed + 3)
        lite = (r > 0.86) & w
        dark = (r < 0.10) & w
        self.step[lite] += 0.75
        self.step[dark] -= 0.75
        # a dark line where the water meets a bank above it (the turf's shadowed underside)
        up = shift(b["mat"], 1, 0, -1)
        edge = w & np.isin(up, [LAND, BOULDER]) & (shift(b["depth"], 1, 0, 0) < b["depth"] + 0.5)
        self.step[edge] -= 1.2

    def rock_marks(self):
        b = self.b
        rk = b["mat"] == BOULDER
        if not rk.any():
            return
        N = b["N"]; L = b["L"]; p = b["p"]; H, W = self.H, self.W
        depth = b["depth"]
        pix_m = depth * 2 * np.tan(np.radians(26)) / W           # metres per pixel
        size_px = self.b_size / np.maximum(pix_m, 1e-6)
        detail = np.clip((size_px - 12) / 50, 0, 1)              # how much texture the rock can carry
        # 1) edges between planes: convex ridges catch the light, creases go dark
        conv = np.zeros((H, W)); cre = np.zeros((H, W))
        for dy, dx in [(0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1), (-1, 0)]:
            Nn = np.stack([shift(N[..., i], dy, dx, 0) for i in range(3)], -1)
            pn = np.stack([shift(p[..., i], dy, dx, 0) for i in range(3)], -1)
            same = shift(rk.astype(int), dy, dx, 0) == 1
            c = np.sum((N - Nn) * (p - pn), -1) / np.maximum(pix_m, 1e-6) ** 2 * pix_m

            conv += np.where(same & (c < 0), ang, 0)             # neighbour turns away: ridge
            cre += np.where(same & (c > 0), ang, 0)
        ndl = np.sum(N * L, -1)
        ridge = rk & (conv > 0.05) & (ndl > 0.05)
        crease = rk & (cre > 0.12)
        self.step[ridge] += 0.8
        self.step[crease] -= 0.9
        # 2) silhouette: a dark accent where the form turns away from the light; a light rim

        sil = np.zeros((H, W), bool)

        self.step[rk] += 0.45 * mott * detail[rk]
        # 4) granite: salt-and-pepper flecks, sparse, 1-2 px clusters, quieter in shadow

        lit = self.step > 2.6

        lt = rk & (np.where(lit, hsh > 0.95, hsh > 0.985)) & (detail > 0.15)
        pair = rk & (hsh2 < 0.03) & lit & (detail > 0.4)

"""Line craft: contours drawn in clean pixel runs.

Measured on Ferrari's mountain skyline (native pixels): vertical steps are almost always
1 px; horizontal run lengths hold steady along a stretch (2,2,2,2 = a 2:1 line; 1,1,1 = 45
deg; 4,4,4) and change gradually round a curve. Ray-marched edges are noisy instead, with
runs like 1,3,1,2 -- 'jaggies'. So every major contour is re-drawn:
  smooth the region's outline -> simplify to a polygon -> snap each edge to a clean ratio
  (flat, 4:1, 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, vertical) -> re-rasterise -> re-fill the pixels
  that changed side from their nearest neighbour on the correct side.
"""
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image, ImageDraw
from scipy import ndimage
from skimage import measure

NICE = [(1, 0), (4, 1), (3, 1), (2, 1), (1, 1), (1, 2), (1, 3), (1, 4), (0, 1)]
DIRS = []
for dx, dy in NICE:
    for sx in (1, -1):
        for sy in (1, -1):
            d = (dx * sx, dy * sy)
            if d not in DIRS:
                DIRS.append(d)
DIRS = np.array(DIRS, float)
DANG = np.arctan2(DIRS[:, 1], DIRS[:, 0])

def snap_polygon(pts, min_len=3.0):
    """walk the polygon; each edge becomes m * (a nice direction), aiming at the original
    next vertex so the drift never accumulates."""
    out = [np.round(pts[0])]
    cur = out[0].copy()
    for i in range(1, len(pts)):
        tgt = pts[i]
        e = tgt - cur
        L = np.hypot(*e)
        if L < min_len:
            cur = np.round(tgt); out.append(cur.copy()); continue
        ang = np.arctan2(e[1], e[0])
        # the two nicest candidate directions around this angle; take the better landing
        diff = np.abs((DANG - ang + np.pi) % (2 * np.pi) - np.pi)
        best = None
        for j in np.argsort(diff)[:2]:
            d = DIRS[j]
            m = max(1, round(np.dot(e, d) / np.dot(d, d)))
            p = cur + m * d
            err = np.hypot(*(p - tgt)) + 0.35 * diff[j] * L
            if best is None or err < best[0]:
                best = (err, p)
        cur = best[1]; out.append(cur.copy())
    return np.array(out)

def clean_mask(mask, sigma=1.0, tol=0.75, min_area=6, snap=True):
    """-> new boolean mask with the same regions, outlines drawn in clean runs."""
    H, W = mask.shape
    pad = 3
    m = np.pad(mask.astype(float), pad, mode="edge")
    sm = ndimage.gaussian_filter(m, sigma)
    out = Image.new("L", (W + 2 * pad, H + 2 * pad), 0)
    dr = ImageDraw.Draw(out)
    lab, n = ndimage.label(mask)
    # outer boundaries fill, holes cut (contours come back oriented; use area sign)
    cs = measure.find_contours(sm, 0.5)
    polys = []
    for c in cs:
        if len(c) < 4:
            continue
        xy = c[:, ::-1]                                     # (x, y)
        poly = measure.approximate_polygon(xy, tolerance=tol)
        if len(poly) < 3:
            continue
        if snap:
            poly = snap_polygon(poly)
        area = 0.5 * np.sum(poly[:-1, 0] * poly[1:, 1] - poly[1:, 0] * poly[:-1, 1])
        if abs(area) < min_area:
            continue
        # decide fill vs hole by sampling the smoothed mask just inside the polygon
        polys.append((abs(area), poly))
    polys.sort(key=lambda t: -t[0])
    for _, poly in polys:
        img = Image.new("L", out.size, 0)
        ImageDraw.Draw(img).polygon([tuple(p) for p in poly], fill=1, outline=1)
        a = np.array(img, bool)
        inside_val = sm[a].mean() if a.any() else 0
        if inside_val >= 0.5:
            out = Image.fromarray(np.maximum(np.array(out), a.astype(np.uint8)))
        else:
            o = np.array(out); o[a & (sm < 0.5)] = 0; out = Image.fromarray(o)
    res = np.array(out, bool)[pad:-pad, pad:-pad]
    return res

def reassign(buf, keys, new_mask, old_mask):
    """pixels that joined the region copy their data from the nearest old-region pixel;
    pixels that left copy from the nearest pixel outside the old region."""
    gained = new_mask & ~old_mask
    lost = old_mask & ~new_mask
    if gained.any():
        _, (iy, ix) = ndimage.distance_transform_edt(~old_mask, return_indices=True)
        for k in keys:
            a = buf[k]
            a[gained] = a[iy[gained], ix[gained]]
    if lost.any():
        _, (iy, ix) = ndimage.distance_transform_edt(old_mask, return_indices=True)
        for k in keys:
            a = buf[k]
            a[lost] = a[iy[lost], ix[lost]]
    return gained.sum(), lost.sum()

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ spruce sprites
def spruce(h, rng, lean=0):
    """A spruce as pixels, built the way Ferrari's are: a 1-px leader with ticks at the tip,
    then tiers of drooping branch strokes (a flat run that steps down once at its end --
    clean 2:1 / 3:1 runs), their length growing down the tree with some raggedness,
    around a solid core. Returns a list of (dy, dx, kind): kind 0 core, 1 branch, 2 tip.
    dy counts upward from the foot (0) to the tip (h-1)."""
    px = []
    tip = max(2, int(h * 0.14))
    half_max = max(1.5, h * rng.uniform(0.17, 0.24))
    for dy in range(h):
        px.append((dy, 0, 2 if dy > h - tip else 0))
    # ticks on the leader
    for dy in range(h - tip, h - 1, 2):
        s = 1 if (dy // 2) % 2 else -1
        px.append((dy, s, 2))
    dy = h - tip
    while dy > 0:
        env = half_max * ((h - dy) / h) ** 0.95
        for s in (-1, 1):
            L = int(round(env * rng.uniform(0.55, 1.15)))
            if L < 1:
                continue
            droop_at = max(1, int(round(L * rng.uniform(0.55, 0.8))))
            for k in range(1, L + 1):
                ddy = 0 if k <= droop_at else -1
                px.append((dy + ddy, s * k, 1))
            if L >= 3 and rng.random() < 0.5:                  # a second, shorter stroke below
                for k in range(1, L - 1):
                    px.append((dy - 1, s * k, 1))
        # core fill between tiers
        core = int(env * 0.45)
        for c in range(-core, core + 1):
            px.append((dy, c, 0))
            px.append((dy - 1, c, 0))
        dy -= rng.choice([2, 2, 3])
    return px

# Lessons from the atelier: a boulder by a tarn at dusk, after Mark Ferrari

I worked value first and colour last. Each stage below says what I learned, points to the images, and says what went wrong. After the stages come the method (the program) and an honest comparison of my sheets with Ferrari's tarn.

Everything is in `~/work/pixelart-studies/pedagogy/atelier/`. To regenerate every sheet, run `nice -n 10 python3 method.py sheets`: four single-thread worker processes, about 4 minutes.

**Research, and its limits.** I studied Ferrari's `ferrari_alpine_tarn_dusk.png` closely:

- palette statistics;
- crops at his native pixel scale (the file is his 634×469 frame doubled);
- measured run lengths of his contours.

I read his Q&A (EffectGames) on:

- palette economy;
- patterned dither for smooth ramps and diffusion dither for chaos;
- colour cycling.

On form and light I used Proko's value families (the terminator, core, reflected light that never reaches the halftone, cast shadow, occlusion). I also read Pedro Medeiros and Slynyrd on clustering and on rocks, and the notes on Kazuo Oga's poster-colour method (big soft masses first, detail last, about 21 colours).

I did **not** look closely at Ferrari's other colour-cycling scenes or at Ghibli plates. Saving images from the web counts as a download, which needs permission I could not ask for in this session, so I only had text descriptions of them. That gap is real and shows in the results (see the comparison).

## Stage 1: light on simple forms (greyscale)

![forms](figs/s1_forms.png)
![low sun](figs/s1_lowsun.png)

The top row of the first sheet is the two-value **notan** (light family against shadow family). The bottom row is the six-value painting.

What I learned:

- **The two families must never touch.** Early versions let ambient occlusion push lit ground into the shadow family. The notan showed a black pool round every base, and the forms stopped reading. The fix is a rule, not a tweak: occlusion may dim the light family, but it only crosses into shadow at a thin contact line. I found the same bug a second time, in its SDF normalisation, when the terrain distance field was scaled. The notan caught it both times.
- **A value plan beats a lighting formula.** Mapping N·L linearly onto greys gave the lit face of the cube the same grey as the ground. What worked was a six-step plan:
  - light: steps 5 and 4;
  - halftone: 3;
  - *(the gap at the terminator)*;
  - reflected light: 2;
  - shadow: 1;
  - core, occlusion and the root of the cast shadow: 0.

  The greys are spaced by eye (0.07, 0.17, 0.27 | 0.52, 0.70, 0.90), with a big jump at the terminator. A *keyed* material like the ground gets its own local value (an albedo) so it turns fewer steps.
- **The core falls out of geometry.** Point the bounce light from below and away from the sun, and the band just past the terminator faces neither light nor bounce. The cylinder then shows light, halftone, terminator, a dark core, a reflected-light strip and the rim, as in any Bargue-style cast drawing.
- **Dither is for turning surfaces, not flat ones.** A flat plane that lands between two greys got a checkerboard, which was ugly. `quantize(flat_snap=…)` rounds wherever the value is not changing, ignoring jumps at plane edges. Curved forms get one checker band per step, which is how Ferrari's hummocks and snowfields turn.
- **A low sun from each direction** (second sheet), the lesson I carried into the task:
  - from the side, the form is split in two;
  - from the front, the form flattens and survives only in the halftone at its edges;
  - from behind, everything sits in the shadow family and only rims can speak (the rims needed a later stage).

## Stage 2: the boulder as form (greyscale)

![first boulders](stage2/boulder_v2_all.png)
![hull boulders](stage2/hull_all.png)

First attempt: a lopsided ellipsoid with planes shaved off, rounded edges and a joint crack. At a distance the notan read. In close-up it read as a **pillow or loaf**: the flat faces and even rounding looked manufactured. One seed read as a skull (two lobes plus a crack).

Second attempt, `HullBoulder`: I stopped drawing an ellipsoid and started drawing *a potato with facets*.

- It takes a dozen points on a lopsided mass, plus a ring of points at the buried waist so the foot is broad (it *sits*, it does not balance).
- The convex hull of those points gives 20–30 facets of **very unequal size**: a few big planes and many small ones.
- A log-sum-exp blend rounds the edges (weathering, since granite exfoliates in shells).
- A gentle low-frequency swell, one or two shallow spalls (concave scoops) and one partial joint finish it.

Sunk about 40% into the ground, it reads as heavy in five or six values: a lit plane, a halftone top, a clear terminator ridge and a quiet shadow.

What I learned:

- **Keep the shadows simple and put the detail in the light.** My first shadow side had reflected-light patches and a core on every facet that faced slightly away. The result was busy and face-like. Weakening the bounce and narrowing the core made the shadow read as one plane.
- **Where the boulder meets the ground decides whether it is heavy.** An ellipsoid's bulge above the ground makes a black occlusion ring, like a rubber base. Sinking the boulder past its widest point, and later growing grass over the foot, turns that ring into a *seat*.

## Stage 3: the setting as form (greyscale)

![value](figs/s3_value_medium_left_10m_0.png) ![notan](figs/s3_notan_medium_left_10m_0.png)
![value](figs/s3_value_large_back_close_1.png) ![notan](figs/s3_notan_large_back_close_1.png)

The first layout put land behind the boulder and water in front of the camera. I had to work out (on a top-down debug map) where the water must be for the boulder's **reflection** to be visible: *between* the camera and the boulder's foot. The final layout is a cove:

- the boulder sits at the tip of a promontory, on the turf lip;
- its front shore curls round to join the bank the camera stands on;
- its far shore recedes back-left;
- the far bank, spruce and mountains lie 150–200 m off.

Being that far away, they live in a *direction-only* backdrop, so their reflection is automatically the mirror image about the horizon, as it is in nature.

What I learned:

- **Design the notan of the whole picture; don't let the renderer choose it.** In the first honest notan, land and water were the same middle value and the picture could not be read. Ferrari's pattern is:
  - light sky and water;
  - a dark band of trees and far bank;
  - middle-value grass;
  - the focal boulder carrying the full range.

  I got there with value decisions, not physics:
  - weaker Fresnel darkening, so the water stays light;
  - grass *keyed*: flat turf sits on the halftone at any sun height, and slopes climb or fall from there (vertical blades catch a low sun that a flat surface would barely get);
  - grass shadows lifted to a mid-dark, because sky fill is strong at dusk.
- **Contrast closes up with distance.** At 40 m the hummock shadows made dark blotches. Aerial perspective, and shadows that lift toward the light with distance, fixed it.
- **Choices I made that the brief did not state:**
  - pitch 9° rather than exactly 10°, which leaves a strip of sky above the far trees;
  - sun elevation 9°;
  - horizontal field of view 52°;
  - "left" means azimuth 22° (side light from a little in front), because at 8° the camera saw almost only the shadow side;
  - "close" means about 2.7 boulder widths, minimum 3.6 m.

## Stage 4: material, still in grey, and line craft

![no marks](figs/s4_nomarks_grey.png)
![marks](figs/s4_marks_grey.png)

Painting happens in *step space*. Every material owns a ramp, and marks are offsets along it. Quantising mixes only two **adjacent** entries of **one** ramp, with a checker dither. Marks that are meant to be single pixels (granite flecks, glints, tufts) go on *after* the stray-pixel cleanup.

- **Granite.** First I tried salt-and-pepper flecks everywhere, at even density. It read as terrazzo or concrete. What read as granite:
  - flecks sparse and gathered in patches (a noise field decides where grains gather);
  - flecks mostly dark, in the light and halftone, and rare in shadow;
  - a mottle that sticks to the stone (object space, not screen space);
  - convex ridges between planes catching light (+1 step);
  - creases and the shadow-side silhouette going dark;
  - under back light, a 2-px rim that thins as the form turns away.
- **Grass.** Vertical strokes whose length is a fixed 20 cm in the world, converted to pixels by depth: long in front, dithered specks at 40 m. The first stroke value mix was too soft and stripy next to Ferrari (his lit grass is about 40% dark olive strokes on ochre), so I made it denser and higher in contrast. Tufts were first a picket fence of identical sticks. They became **clumps of 3–6 blades fanning from one foot**, each blade a clean 1:2, 1:3 or 1:4 line, tallest in the middle, with lit tips. Grass round the boulder's foot takes its light from the stone behind it.
- **Water.** A broken reflection (rows wobble sideways, more toward the viewer), sparse ripple dashes that thin toward the far shore, one or two catspaw bands, and a dark line under every bank. Under back light, a glitter path of glint pixels widens toward the viewer. The glint colour is reserved for the sun.

![granite and grass](figs/s4_zoom_granite_grass.png)
![water and trees](figs/s4_zoom_water_trees.png)

### Line craft (the critic's note)

The critic pointed out that the pixel medium favours short straight runs and clean 45° and 2:1 steps. I measured Ferrari's mountain skyline at native scale:

- vertical steps are almost always **1 px**;
- horizontal runs **hold steady along a stretch** (2,2,2,2 is a 2:1 line; 1,1,1 is 45°; 4,4,4 is 4:1);
- runs change **gradually** round a curve (6,5,4,4,4).

My ray-marched edges gave noisy runs like 1,3,1,2. `lines.py` redraws every major contour: the bank against the water, the boulder, their reflections, the skyline and the snowline. It smooths the outline, simplifies it to a polygon and snaps each edge to a clean ratio (flat, 4:1, 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, and the steep equivalents), always aiming at the original next vertex so drift cannot build up. It then re-rasterises and refills the changed pixels from their nearest neighbour on the correct side. Snapping can fold an edge back on itself, so one-pixel slivers are removed.

![before](figs/lines_mtn_before.png)
![after](figs/lines_mtn_after.png)
![boulder before](figs/lines_before.png) ![boulder after](figs/lines_after.png)

The note also changed how I *build* things, not only how I clean them:

- **Mountains** are now a few massifs with straight flanks (2:1, 1:1, 3:1 slopes). Their facets fan from each summit along clean ratios, and the snowline is a triangle wave, so the couloirs are clean zigzags. My noise-based mountains before this looked like curtains, scratches and, at one point, a city skyline.
- **Spruce** are built from pixel strokes, the way his are: a 1-px leader with ticks at the tip, then tiers of drooping branch strokes (a flat run that steps down once at its end) round a solid core, with lit branch ends on the sun side.

## Stage 5: colour last

![palette](sheets/palette_card.png)
![grey](figs/s4_marks_grey.png)
![colour](figs/s5_colour.png)

The palette has 32 hand-placed colours (`palette.py`). Each colour was chosen to hit the luminance its ramp position had already proved in grey; the palette card shows each colour above its own grey. Ramps are lists of palette indices, so they can **share**: the water ramp borrows the rock's and mountains' violets and the sky's lilacs, which is honest, since the water is mostly reflecting them. That sharing freed room for a deep peaty violet and one reserved glint.

The dusk logic, taken from Ferrari's own colours (sampled from the tarn painting):

- **Light is warm:** peach, orange, ochre, cream.
- **Shadow is cool and violet**, because the pink and violet sky fills it.
- **Greens are pushed to olive and ochre**, with near-black olive in the darkest turf.
- **Snow in shade uses the sky's lilacs; snow in sun, the sky's creams.** Snow is a mirror of the sky; giving it the mountain ramp gave rose-coloured snow in shade, which was wrong.
- **Trees go to near-black brown-olive**, with bronze lit needles.

Colour changed no value decision. The grey and colour versions have the same structure, which was the point of the order.

|---|---|---|
| geometry | `geometry.py` | `HullBoulder` (lopsided hull, weathered edges, spalls, joint, sunk 38%); `Setting` (cove shoreline in the boulder's view frame, hummocks, turf lip, near bank) |

## Honest comparison with the reference

![grass](figs/compare_grass.png)

Ferrari is on the left, my sheet cell on the right, both at the same pixel scale.

**What holds up:**

- The value architecture reads in every cell and under every light: a light sky and water, a dark tree band, middle-value grass and a focal boulder. Back-lit cells work: glowing sky, dark silhouettes, a cream rim on the stone, glowing grass and a glitter path.
- The boulder reads as heavy, faceted and seated, and the granite reads as granite at close and 10 m range. The reflection hangs beneath it.
- The palette is warm light over violet shadow, with olive and ochre grass, and is recognisably from the same family as his.
- The contours are drawn in clean runs, the spruce are built like his, and the mountain flanks step in 1:1 and 2:1.

**Where it falls short, plainly:**

1. **Texture density.** Ferrari's surfaces are textured *everywhere*: dither is a texture in its own right, not only a transition, and the grass, trees and snow all vibrate. My large areas (the middle water, the lit mountain faces, much of the sky) are flat fields with a transition band. His picture is richer by a wide margin; mine reads cleaner and more graphic, closer to a modern pixel-art poster than to his painterly density.
2. **Water.** His water is the richest part of the tarn painting: a full, detailed, slightly darkened mirror of mountains and trees. Mine reflects correctly, but at 9° down most of my water mirrors the plain upper sky, so a lot of the frame is lilac with ripple dashes. The Fresnel gradient and catspaws help but do not solve it.
3. **Hummocks.** His banks are big rounded mounds that turn from lit ochre to olive shadow through long checker gradients (the island in the tarn). My bank's hummocks are low and read only as texture. I never got a mound to read the way his do.
4. **Trees.** Mine are clean sprites; his have dense, irregular needle texture with bronze light inside the mass, not only at the edges.
5. **Composition.** Every cell uses the same cove recipe, so the sheets are consistent but samey. At 40 m the boulder is honestly small (about 12 px) and the land spit dominates.
6. **Research gap.** I studied one Ferrari painting in depth and knew his other scenes and Ghibli backgrounds only from text. The lessons above are learned against that one painting.

If I had another week, I would put it into items 1 to 3, in that order: dither as texture across the flat masses, richer reflections (lower the water's share of the frame, or give the sky more structure to mirror), and mounds built on purpose as forms in the bank.
