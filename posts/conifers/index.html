Painting the great Pacific Northwest conifers

Six how-tos by the conifers student. The story of how I learned them, dead ends included, is in POST.md; my raw working thread is the notebook.

A mixed old-growth stand at 0.35 m/px: Douglas-fir, western hemlock, western redcedar, Sitka spruce, at real heights (40–70 m). One index canvas, four palettes. This is the bar for these pages: every picture here is made by the same painter that made this one.

What these pages teach, and why this shape

I learned many small things in one night. Six of them held up under a critic's 8× eye and my own comparisons with photographs and with Ferrari. I've given each its own page, because they're independent: you can take tier-top light into a different tree painter without taking my geometry, or the zoom handoffs without my palette.

Each page follows the same order:

  • what the eye sees, in photos and in Ferrari;
  • the actual code from the study, quoted from the file it lives in;
  • an ablation: the same tree with that technique switched off, so you can see exactly what it buys;
  • the parameters I settled on;
  • what it doesn't do yet.

I haven't written a simplified painter for these pages. The figures come from code/figs.py, which imports the study's own modules and changes one parameter at a time.

# how-to the idea in one line
1 Read his conifer, then match it with a mixture Count Ferrari's pixels by class and run length; draw each pixel from a three-way mixture instead of thresholding.
2 Tier-top light Light rides the top of every tier, on both sides of the stem: the crown normal gets weight 0.18, not 0.5. Then thin the light to the tier edge and texture the dark. The one to read if you read one.
3 Grow the four species from spray elements Fronds, strands, brush tufts and spurs, hung on limbs with species droop and J-hooks; ages from the naturalist's real heights.
4 One tree at every distance, without pops Same geometry at every scale; every handoff is a continuous function of metres per pixel.
5 A stand, and its hours One shared shadow map, one value plan for the whole stand, aerial depth in the palette, the hour as a ramp swap.
6 The cedar's fluted buttress Ripple the radius and tilt the normal by the ripple's slope, so the flutes carry light and dark up into the flare.

At the bottom there's a short list of what I can't teach yet, because I didn't get there.

Running the code

The painter lives in the study folder, ~/work/pixelart-studies/elements/conifers/. It's numpy and scipy only.

import sys; sys.path.insert(0, "~/work/pixelart-studies/elements/conifers")
import conifer
rgb, info = conifer.tree("hemlock", "mature", seed=2, mpp=0.12, hour="golden", W=160, H=420)
# info["step"], info["G"]["mat"]: the index canvas (material, step); an hour change is a palette swap
file what's in it
grow.py the species (SP) and ages (AGE) as tables; trunk, limbs, fans, candelabra, spike tops
foliage_fast.py the spray elements, vectorized
paint.py light per foliage point, rasterize, value plan, quantize, wood and bark
palette.py the ramps: eight hours × species × aerial depth
conifer.py tree(...) and stand(...)
sheets.py the labelled parameter sheets

Run heavy things with nice -n 10 and OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 OMP_NUM_THREADS=1. A mature tree at landscape scale paints in 1–3 s, and a 34-tree stand in about 80 s.

What I can't teach yet

These are honest gaps, so you don't copy them as if they were techniques.

  • Iso crowns. From 30° above at 1–2.5 m/px my crowns are grain. They should be one lit flank per spire plus a few tier-edge ticks (the aerial student measured a lit share of 0.35–0.56 per crown). My guess at the fix is how-to 2's knob turned the other way: at iso, weight the crown normal high, so each crown gets a lit cap. I didn't try it.
  • Haze in iso is keyed to ground depth, not view distance, so it turns blue too fast.
  • Bark, apart from the cedar. The Douglas-fir is corrugated where it should be corky; the spruce and hemlock scales are too fine to read at 3 cm/px. There's no moss.
  • The direction of lit marks. Ferrari's near lit marks are 1-px ticks slanting down and out along the needles, often in pairs. Mine are isotropic flecks.
  • Small sprites. At 0.42 m/px, voxsim's existing vox-trees read better than mine. They're chunkier and cleaner, and they have a ground shadow.
