Painting land from above: a field guide

By the aerial student. The story of how I learned this is in POST.md; my raw working thread is the notebook.

This isn't one recipe. Land from above is many different things at many different sizes, and what I learned is that each one needs its own technique, chosen by how many pixels it is. So this is a field guide: the numbers I measured on oblique aerial photographs, and a set of how-tos, one per thing, each taught from the code that painted my plates (~/work/pixelart-studies/aerial/lib/) and the study image that proved it. Each how-to says where it fell short.

The far view at evening: the photo it was judged against, and mine. 320×180, 64 colours, shown at 3×.

The one idea

Choose the technique by pixel size, and look at the photo at that same pixel size before you paint. A conifer 10 px wide is a tiered stroke. At 4 px it's a lit arrowhead over a dark notch. At 1 px it's part of a surface, carried by a sparse stipple. The photo tells you which, but only if you shrink it to the size it will have in your frame:

im.resize((width, round(h * width / w)), Image.BOX)     # the photo at your frame's pixel size

The same forest from 0.3 to 4.8 m/px: spur strokes, then spire marks, then the canopy surface. It changes technique three times without a visible pop. (The pale strip around the last cell is the edge of the 700 m world, not sky.)

The numbers (measured on oblique photos)

These are what later went into voxsim's renderer. How I measured them is in how-to 1.

what measured photos
conifer crown spacing ÷ lit-cap radius 2.2–2.9 (closed stands 2.3–2.5) Deer Mtn, Alsek, NRCS, Cosumnes
lit share of a canopy 0.35–0.56: mostly lit caps, narrow dark seams same
contrast inside one crown up to 46 L* (sunlit oak 70 vs 24), while the squinted mass is flat Cosumnes
crowns on screen, taller ÷ wider conifers 1.25–1.5, broadleaf 0.4–0.8 same
a river seen steeply from above L* 23, colourless: darker than canopy (53) and fields (68) Cosumnes
the same river at grazing angles L* 45, chroma 32: sky Cosumnes
a tarn from above L* 46, blue Sawtooth
rock: lit vs shade 85 warm vs 51 blue (hue 67° vs 245°) Sawtooth
shaded snow L* 76, 25 above shaded rock Sawtooth
haze from near to far (high oblique) black point 0.5 → 48, value range 45 → 9, b* 0 → −14 Blue Ridge
dawn canopy median L* 8: the photos really are that dark Deer Mtn

The how-tos

  1. Look at a photo at its pixel size, and measure it. The habit that made everything else work: box-downsampling, box tones, crown detection at the dominant scale, and haze row by row. It includes the detector that lied to me.
  2. Forest from above, by crown size. The best thing I made, and the one that transferred. Spur strokes on a lit-cap gradient, spire marks, and the canopy as a lit surface. Cast shadows. The slope read through the canopy.
  3. Water as a dark mirror. Rivers and tarns by tracing the reflected ray, with a Fresnel stylisation you choose on purpose. Then the coast, zoned by depth, with surf as crest and lace.
  4. The far view: haze, bands and palette economy. How the evening range was made: depth bands with dithered seams, haze mixed into each band, band collapse, cavity tone, a palette budget, and one index canvas re-lit for every hour.
  5. Mountain ground: slopes, rock, scree, cliffs and snow. Slope tone as posterised planes, rock facets stretched along the fall line, stones on a quiet matrix, ledges, and snow drawn in its shadows. My results here are weaker, and the page says where.
  6. Small things: huts and fire. A hut stands up by its shadow and its two roof planes.

Running the code

Everything runs from the study folder, 4 worker processes, one thread each:

cd ~/work/pixelart-studies/aerial
nice -n 10 python3 study_canopy.py      # studies/canopy.png   (also study_river, study_mountain, study_objects)
nice -n 10 python3 plates.py [name ...] [--big]
nice -n 10 python3 sheets.py [scale|light|seed|far]

The figures that are new on these pages come from code/figs.py. It imports the study's lib/ read-only and changes nothing in it.

The painter's scaffold is thin, and the how-tos lean on it, so here it is in one paragraph. terrain.py ray-marches a heightfield into a G-buffer (world xyz, normal, distance, metres per pixel, sun visibility). Each technique writes into an index canvas of (material, form step, depth band) (canvas.py). The palette is the light: pal.py computes each material's ramp from a measured albedo and a light state, with the lower half of every ramp the shadow family and the upper half the lit family, and dither never crosses between them. The same canvas re-lights for any hour by swapping the palette. Everything else on these pages is a technique.

What I'd still fix

  • The forested slope plate reads as an even carpet. The photo reads by its ridge, meadows and tracks. That's the scene's job, not the painter's, but it's missing.
  • At 640×360, crowns over ~15 px show "venetian blind" stripes from regular tier spacing.
  • My crowns are ~15% too tall (1.44–1.75 against 1.25–1.5).
  • Far snow and the cirque floor are the weakest parts (how-to 5).
  • The evening forest needed a lifted sky ambient, a painter's choice rather than a measured one (how-to 4).
